# Review 1 research note: results and defensible next experiments

**Prepared 19 October 2026.** This is a research and presentation note, not a new experiment. The numerical results below come from the committed [forecast metrics](../artifacts/forecast_metrics.csv), [paired differences](../results/paper/paired_differences.csv), and [findings](../results/paper/FINDINGS.md). The method and its limits are specified in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md). No change proposed here has yet been measured on the held-out test days.

## What the existing result actually says

| Question | Current evidence | Accurate interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Does the GCN–GRU forecast better? | Mean three-horizon test MAE: GCN–GRU **0.4893**, matched no-graph GRU **0.4834**, persistence **0.4858** Mbps. On validation, GCN–GRU beats persistence; on test, it does not. It does beat persistence narrowly at horizon 3 alone (0.6079 versus 0.6121 Mbps). | No overall held-out forecast advantage for the graph component. The validation-to-test reversal merits diagnosis; it does not establish the cause. |
| Does predictive routing beat reactive carbon routing? | With congested load and a one-bin observation lag: proposed **114** versus reactive **126** overload minutes/day (9.5% fewer). Served fraction: **91.92%** versus **91.89%**. | A conditional congestion gain in this simulator. Five adjacent days and one model seed limit inference. |
| Does it improve the carbon objective? | In that same comparison, forwarding score is **333.18** versus **323.83** (2.9% worse; lower is better). | No. The score is a served-byte-weighted attribution under assumed equal energy per hop, **not** measured grams of CO₂. |
| Is the GNN responsible for the routing gain? | Persistence with identical safeguards also gives **114** overload minutes/day. The GNN policy has a lower score than persistence (333.18 versus 338.58) but neither reaches reactive carbon's 323.83. | Current evidence supports the conservative routing design more than a distinct GNN benefit. |

This distinction matters: reducing forecast MAE and improving the **carbon–congestion routing frontier** are separate research problems. Lower MAE need not translate into better routing; the original study of traffic engineering with estimated matrices found that error magnitude alone was a poor predictor of routing performance ([Roughan, Thorup, Zhang, IMC 2003](https://doi.org/10.1145/948205.948237)). Carbon-aware routing research also stresses the dependence of any claimed emissions benefit on the power model and performance constraints ([El-Zahr et al., *Exploring the Benefits of Carbon-Aware Routing*, CoNEXT 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3629165)).

## Why an improvement is plausible, without pretending it is guaranteed

The current [planner](../src/carbonroute/routing.py) is greedy: for each OD pair it chooses the least-carbon feasible candidate, checks that candidate's own links against an 80% utilization ceiling, and only then applies hold-time and change thresholds. Its carbon ranking is per-path, while the reported score is **served-byte weighted over all flows**. If several feasible assignments exist, independent path choices need not minimize network-wide carbon or congestion. The current six OD pairs each have exactly two delay-feasible paths, yielding only `2^6 = 64` joint assignments. This is a project-specific opportunity, not a claim that brute-force search scales to a large network. Prior green traffic engineering explicitly optimizes an environmental goal *under* utilization and delay constraints ([GreenTE, ICNP 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICNP.2010.5762751)); CATE also treats carbon-aware routing as a traffic-engineering problem rather than a per-path label alone ([El-Zahr et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3629165)).

The current [forecast model](../src/carbonroute/prediction.py) receives twelve 15-minute OD matrices, aggregates neighboring node features through one fixed physical adjacency matrix, and predicts three bins ahead. Only six OD series are active. Physical proximity may not be the strongest dependency among OD demands: Graph WaveNet's original paper motivates *learned* spatial dependencies when the fixed graph misses relationships ([Wu et al., IJCAI 2019](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/2019/0264.pdf)). That motivates a small ablation, **not** a promise that a larger GNN will win on this small trace. Graph forecasting precedent is established by [DCRNN (ICLR 2018)](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=SJiHXGWAZ), so generic “first ST-GNN for routing” wording is unsafe. Predictive SDN routing already exists in [NeuRoute (CNSM 2017)](https://dl.ifip.org/db/conf/cnsm/cnsm2017/1570395499.pdf), and carbon-intensity forecasting for routing already exists in [CIRo (e-Energy 2023)](https://netsec.ethz.ch/publications/papers/green_routing2023.pdf).

The safety margin is the 90th percentile of **pooled** positive validation residuals, per horizon. A single allowance may over-reserve consistently easy flows and under-reserve hard ones. Its near-90% coverage on the *normal* test series does not prove coverage under scaled congestion or synthetic bursts. A per-OD, per-horizon margin, or a simple load-stratified margin with adequate validation sample counts, could change the congestion–carbon trade-off. Treat these as empirical calibration schemes; classical split conformal guarantees rely on conditions such as exchangeability that time-series shifts may violate ([Lei et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.04173); [Zaffran et al., ICML 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/zaffran22a/zaffran22a.pdf)).

## Evaluation rule before changing the implementation

1. Keep the existing `results/paper/` campaign frozen and labeled **reference v1**. New policies and model variants write to new result folders. Record config, source, model, and input hashes as the existing pipeline does.
2. Use the chronological training split for model fitting and graph/feature learning. Use a **separate part of the current validation period** for model selection and another for residual-margin calibration, or perform a predeclared rolling-origin validation. If the entire validation set is repeatedly searched, selection can overfit it. Rolling-origin, multiple-period evaluation is standard forecast practice ([Tashman, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0169-2070(00)00065-0)).
3. Choose at most a small fixed grid of carbon budgets, margin quantiles, and one forecast change based on validation-period *paired* results, and state the primary endpoint in advance. A reasonable aim is to lower overload **subject to a predeclared carbon-score tolerance**, while reporting served fraction and route changes. Do not tune one parameter to MAE and then infer it must improve routing.
4. Freeze model, margins, path selector, tie rules, budgets, scenarios, and metrics before one final held-out evaluation. Report *all* planned test cases, including negative outcomes. Because existing test results have already been inspected, honestly label future changes as **post-observation development** and, for a stronger paper claim, evaluate the frozen revision on later untouched days or another predeclared temporal holdout. Repeating runs with different labels is not independent evidence.
5. Add multiple neural seeds and disjoint test days when time permits. Bootstrap over day clusters, not 15-minute bins; the current five adjacent days remain a small sample. Keep baseline information sets identical and never give any policy future actual demand or future carbon.

## Suggested one-minute Review 1 statement

> We have a working eight-node GÉANT trace-driven simulation using public traffic and European carbon datasets. The GCN–GRU does **not** yet improve mean held-out forecast accuracy over persistence or a matched GRU. In congested replay with a one-bin observation lag, the combined predictive routing system cuts overload from 126 to 114 minutes per day relative to guarded reactive carbon routing, but raises our carbon-weighted forwarding score by 2.9%; a persistence-based variant attains the same overload. So our current finding is a measurable carbon–congestion trade-off, not a claim of GNN superiority or physical emission savings. Our next controlled experiment is a network-wide carbon-budgeted path selector and better validation-calibrated demand margins. We will freeze choices before another held-out evaluation and report if the simple baselines remain stronger.
